# CASE-003 — Same Brain: Persistence Despite Evidence · Synthesis

> **Rating:** STRONG — with an explicit **scope condition** (see below).

## The parallel in one sentence

The immature guild leader who won't change the failing raid strategy and the
seasoned CEO who won't abandon the failing acquisition reason **the same way** —
because both occupy a **lightly-accountable seat they have publicly staked their
identity on**, and that structural position (not their age, maturity, or IQ)
manufactures the same self-justifying, feedback-resistant cognition.

## Why this is the project's keystone case

CASE-001 (coercion) and CASE-002 (gatekeeping) describe *what* bad leadership does.
CASE-003 describes *why the leader can't see it isn't working* — it is the
**cognitive engine underneath both**. The healer-coercing GM and the gear-gatekeeping
raid leader keep going for the same reason the escalating CEO does: admitting the
approach is failing means admitting *they* failed, and the seat's power and
insulation make that admission feel unnecessary.

## The shared mechanism — and why it's structural, not personal

The observation's sharpest claim is that the cognition is **independent of the
person** (a "9-year-old" and a C-level exec produce it alike). The research explains
exactly why:

1. **Escalation runs on *personal responsibility*, an identity variable the seat
   supplies.** Staw (1976) and the Sleesman (2012) meta-analysis both isolate
   *ownership of the prior decision* — not economics — as what drives doubling down.
   Whoever sits in the seat *owns the calls*, so the seat generates the escalation.
2. **Power itself degrades feedback processing.** Keltner et al. (2003): elevated
   power shifts anyone toward confident, disinhibited, perspective-discounting
   cognition. This is position-induced and general to *any* powerholder.
3. **Held long and unconstrained, it may harden into hubris.** Owen & Davidson
   (2009) frame hubris as **acquired from the exposure to power**, not a pre-existing
   trait — power as pathogen, office as vector.
4. **Selection compounds it.** Grijalva et al. (2015): narcissism predicts getting
   the seat (*emergence*) but not doing well in it (*effectiveness*). So the pipeline
   *selects* self-promoting, correction-resistant people **into** the seat — then the
   seat's power and insulation do the rest. The explanation is **selection + structure**,
   which is why it doesn't require the person to be a diagnosable narcissist.

Personality (narcissism, overconfidence) **modulates the intensity** — but the base
pattern is produced by the configuration of the role. That is the whole point of the
"same brain across a 40-year age gap" observation: change the *person* and you still
get the behavior; change the *accountability structure* and it changes.

## Where it holds (the tight zone)

The equivalence is tightest where the seat is **most powerful and least checked**:
- **WoW:** the single-account GM with near-absolute mechanical control (Poor &
  Skoric 2014; *Massively OP* 2023) and public ego-investment in the guild's strategy.
- **Corporate:** founder-CEOs, dual chair/CEO roles, insider boards — precisely the
  conditions under which Hayward & Hambrick (1997) found hubris effects **strongest.**

## Where it breaks (the scope condition — keep this front and center)

This is the case most vulnerable to over-claiming, so the boundaries are explicit:

1. **Selection/survivorship bias.** Writing about *failure* over-samples villains.
   Plenty of guild leaders (and executives) delegate, plan succession, and adapt.
   The claim is about a **failure mode the structure reliably produces in susceptible
   individuals**, *not* "most leaders are narcissists."
2. **Persistence is sometimes correct.** Duckworth's "grit" predicts real success;
   from the outside, admirable perseverance and irrational escalation look identical.
   Real disconfirming evidence is usually **noisy and ambiguous**, not the clean
   "pure logic and observable results" the observation invokes — so the claim must be
   about *ignoring genuinely diagnostic signals*, not about ever persisting.
3. **De-escalation is achievable.** Sivanathan et al. (2008): protect the ego by
   other means (self-affirmation on *irrelevant* traits) and people abandon losing
   courses. The behavior is a **self-justification response to identity threat** —
   which *confirms* the structural reading (change the conditions, change the
   behavior) while proving it isn't inevitable in any given person.
4. **Accountability blunts it.** The same studies that find the effect find it
   **shrinks under real oversight** — so the CEO is not *fully* "unaccountable," and
   the parallel is weakest where governance actually bites.

## Rating justification

**STRONG.** The mechanism match is unusually tight and **heavily evidenced on the
corporate side** (foundational + meta-analytic: Staw, Sleesman, Chatterjee &
Hambrick, Roll, Hayward & Hambrick, Keltner, Hambrick & Mason), and the two
moderators the literature keeps surfacing — **personal responsibility for the prior
decision** and **weak accountability** — are exactly the structural variables the
observation names. The rating is held at STRONG *because* the scope condition is
stated, not in spite of it: the defensible claim is "the unaccountable, identity-
invested seat reliably produces this failure mode," which the evidence supports. The
WoW side is thinner (practitioner columns + guild-collapse studies, snippet-verified)
and leans on the corporate mechanism for the "why," so a case could be made for
PARTIAL; STRONG is justified by how cleanly the structural variables line up on both
sides and by the community's *own* rejection of the age-based explanation.
