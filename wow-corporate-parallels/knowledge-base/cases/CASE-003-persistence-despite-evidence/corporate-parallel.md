# CASE-003 — Same Brain: Persistence Despite Evidence · Corporate Parallel

> **Status:** drafted

## The corporate/organizational equivalent

The C-suite version of the "9-year-old narcissistic guild leader who won't change
the wipe strat" is a **deeply studied phenomenon** with its own decades-long
research literatures. Executives persist with failing strategies despite
disconfirming evidence for reasons that are, tellingly, **not about being an
executive** — they are general features of *holding a lightly-accountable position
you have publicly staked your identity on.* That is precisely why the guild leader
and the CEO converge.

Five mechanisms, each evidenced:

## 1. Escalation of commitment / sunk cost — persisting *because* you own the choice

- **Staw (1976), "Knee-Deep in the Big Muddy," *Org. Behavior and Human
  Performance* 16(1):27–44.** DOI 10.1016/0030-5073(76)90005-2. [EVIDENCE]
  Decision-makers commit *more* to a failing course when they were **personally
  responsible** for the original decision. Negative feedback *increases* commitment
  when you own the prior choice — isolating **self-justification** from economics.
  → This is the raid leader running the wipe strat again, mechanized.
- **Arkes & Blumer (1985), *OBHDP* 35(1):124–140.** DOI 10.1016/0749-5978(85)90049-4.
  [EVIDENCE] Sunk cost is a **general human default** (desire not to appear
  wasteful), not an executive trait — supporting "structure/position, not person."
- **Staw & Ross (1987), "Behavior in Escalation Situations," *Research in Org.
  Behavior* 9:39–78.** [EVIDENCE — review] A four-class model of persistence —
  **project, psychological (self-justification), social (face-saving),
  organizational/structural** — with social/structural forces dominating later. →
  The theoretical spine: the *structural* forces generalize from guild to boardroom.
- **Sleesman, Conlon, McNamara & Miles (2012), "Cleaning Up the Big Muddy," *AMJ*
  55(3):541–562.** DOI 10.5465/amj.2010.0696. [EVIDENCE — meta-analysis, 379 effect
  sizes] **Personal responsibility for the prior decision** is a robust amplifier
  across decades. → The quantitative backbone for "identity investment → persistence."

## 2. CEO / executive narcissism — bold, erratic, feedback-resistant

- **Chatterjee & Hambrick (2007), "It's All About Me," *Administrative Science
  Quarterly* 52(3):351–386.** DOI 10.2189/asqu.52.3.351. [EVIDENCE — archival, 111
  CEOs] Narcissism (measured via photo prominence, first-person-singular pronouns,
  relative pay) predicts **grandiose, dynamic strategy, bigger acquisitions, and
  more extreme/fluctuating performance** (big wins *and* losses) — **not** better
  average performance — and **less responsiveness to objective/social feedback.**
  → The executive twin of the GM who won't change comp and ignores the logs.
- **Maccoby (2000), "Narcissistic Leaders," *HBR*.** [OPINION] "Productive
  narcissists" bring vision but share flaws: hypersensitivity to criticism, poor
  listening, and **refusing to heed warnings** while isolating from dissent.

## 3. Hubris / overconfidence — and it's *worse where oversight is weak*

- **Roll (1986), "The Hubris Hypothesis of Corporate Takeovers," *J. Business*
  59(2):197–216.** DOI 10.1086/296325. [EVIDENCE/theory] Managers over-trust their
  own valuations and systematically overpay — hubris, not synergy, explains premiums.
- **Hayward & Hambrick (1997), *ASQ* 42(1):103–127.** DOI 10.2307/2393810. [EVIDENCE]
  CEO hubris (recent success, media praise, relative pay) predicts **larger
  acquisition premiums** — and the effect is **stronger when board oversight is
  weak** (inside directors, CEO also chair), followed by **shareholder losses.**
  → Operationalizes "**unaccountable** authority" as a measured moderator: the
  guild-leader/CEO equivalence is tightest exactly where accountability is weakest.
- **Malmendier & Tate (2005), "CEO Overconfidence and Corporate Investment," *J.
  Finance* 60(6):2661–2700.** DOI 10.1111/j.1540-6261.2005.00813.x. [EVIDENCE]
  Overconfident CEOs distort real capital allocation in stable, measurable ways.
- **Moore & Healy (2008), "The Trouble With Overconfidence," *Psych. Review*
  115(2):502–517.** DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.115.2.502. [EVIDENCE] The dangerous
  flavor is **overprecision** — unwarranted certainty that *survives disconfirming
  data.* → Names precisely the "results don't reach the decision" property.

## 4. Why the *seat* shapes cognition (the structural core)

- **Hambrick & Mason (1984), "Upper Echelons," *AMR* 9(2):193–206.** DOI
  10.5465/amr.1984.4277628. [EVIDENCE — foundational] Executives act on a
  **personalized, filtered construal** of the situation, not the situation itself.
  → Strategy is routed through whoever holds the top seat.
- **Keltner, Gruenfeld & Anderson (2003), "Power, Approach, and Inhibition," *Psych.
  Review* 110(2):265–284.** DOI 10.1037/0033-295X.110.2.265. [EVIDENCE] **Power
  itself** activates approach — reward focus, heuristic (less deliberate) cognition,
  disinhibition, **discounting others' perspectives.** → The mechanistic heart:
  power degrades attention to disconfirming social information in *any* powerholder.
  The guild leader and the CEO are the same organism under the same lever.
- **Owen & Davidson (2009), "Hubris Syndrome," *Brain* 132(5):1396–1406.** DOI
  10.1093/brain/awp008. [EVIDENCE-adjacent] Proposes hubris as an **acquired**
  pattern emerging *after* extended exposure to substantial, unconstrained power. →
  **Power as the pathogen, the office as the vector** — the strongest single
  statement of the structural thesis (treat as serious hypothesis, not DSM diagnosis).

## 5. The machinery that lets them *not see* the results

- **Nickerson (1998), "Confirmation Bias," *Review of General Psychology*
  2(2):175–220.** DOI 10.1037/1089-2680.2.2.175. [EVIDENCE — review] Once you own a
  strategy, ordinary confirmation bias makes disconfirming data harder to see/weight.
- **Staw, McKechnie & Puffer (1983), "The Justification of Organizational
  Performance," *ASQ* 28(4):582–600.** DOI 10.2307/2393010. [EVIDENCE — archival]
  **Self-serving attribution:** management credits wins to itself, blames losses on
  externals — the attributional escape hatch that keeps identity investment intact.
- **Janis (1972/1982), *Groupthink*.** [OPINION — case-based] Cohesive, insulated
  groups under a directive leader suppress dissent; "mindguards" shield the leader
  from bad news. → The social filter around the seat (the guild-officer clique; the
  yes-man C-suite).

## How the dynamic operates at work

1. A leader makes a strategy call and **publicly owns it** (identity investment).
2. The role concentrates **power and lightly-checked authority** in them.
3. Power biases cognition toward confident action and **away from disconfirming
   feedback** (Keltner); confirmation bias and self-serving attribution reframe the
   bad results; the surrounding group filters out messengers (Janis).
4. Sunk cost + self-justification make abandoning the course feel like admitting
   error, so negative feedback **increases** commitment (Staw).
5. The behavior is *worse* where oversight is weak (Hayward & Hambrick) — which is
   why founder-CEOs, dual chair/CEO roles, and single-account GMs are the extreme
   cases. Personality (narcissism, overconfidence) **modulates intensity** but does
   not, alone, carry the explanation.

## What corroborates / what complicates

- **Supports the parallel:** every mechanism is evidenced, several with archival
  executive data; and the two moderators the literature keeps surfacing — **personal
  responsibility for the prior decision** and **weak accountability** — are *exactly*
  the structural variables the guild-leader observation points to.
- **Complicates it:** (see `synthesis.md` for the full scope condition)
  - De-escalation is achievable (Sivanathan et al. 2008) — persistence isn't fixed.
  - Some persistence is rational (Duckworth's "grit"); from outside, admirable
    perseverance and irrational escalation look identical, and real disconfirming
    evidence is usually noisy, not "clear."
  - **Base rate:** narcissism predicts leader *emergence* but not *effectiveness*
    (Grijalva et al. 2015, meta-analysis) — most executives aren't clinical
    narcissists, so a narcissism-only story is too narrow. This actually *supports*
    the structural reading: **selection into the seat**, plus the seat, does the work.
