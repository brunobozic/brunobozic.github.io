# CASE-005 — Deniable Enforcement · Corporate Parallel

> **Status:** drafted

## The corporate/organizational equivalent

This is the case with the **tightest single academic match in the whole project.**
"Make the other raiders do the policing so leadership's fingerprints disappear" is,
almost word-for-word, **concertive control** — the landmark finding that when
hierarchy is replaced by peer enforcement, the peers police each other *harder* than
management would, while management stops being the visible enforcer. Around that spine
sit four evidenced literatures matching the four guild mechanisms.

## 1. Outsourced peer policing → **concertive / clan / normative control**

- **Barker (1993), "Tightening the Iron Cage," *ASQ* 38(3):408–437.** DOI
  10.2307/2393374. [EVIDENCE — longitudinal ethnography] When a firm replaced
  supervisors with self-managing teams, control didn't loosen — workers built
  value-based rules and **policed each other more punitively than a manager legally
  could or would.** The cage was drawn *tighter*, with **no manager's fingerprints on
  it.** The load-bearing citation for the entire case.
- **Tompkins & Cheney (1985)** coined **concertive/unobtrusive control** — control
  that is real but *has no visible controller* (built-in deniability). [EVIDENCE/theory]
- **Ouchi (1979/1980), clan control**, *Management Science* 25(9) / *ASQ* 25(1). DOIs
  10.1287/mnsc.25.9.833, 10.2307/2392231. [EVIDENCE/theory] Governing by internalized
  shared values (vs. rules or prices) is a deliberate **design choice** — which peers
  then enforce. Fits where output is hard to measure (like "dedication").
- **Kunda (1992), *Engineering Culture*** — **normative control**: engineering members'
  "minds and hearts" so commitment substitutes for supervision. [EVIDENCE/opinion] The
  affective fuel that makes peers *willing* to police each other. (Also anchors CASE-001.)
- **Sewell (1998), "The Discipline of Teams," *ASQ* 43(2):397–428.** [EVIDENCE] Names
  **horizontal/peer surveillance** explicitly: management supplies the *data* (who's
  contributing), peers apply the *pressure* — enforcement outsourced, oversight
  retained. (WoW analog: the raid's damage-meter/surveillance addons.)

## 2. Manufactured peer pressure → **conformity, mobbing, ostracism**

- **Asch (1955)** conformity: ~75% conform to a unanimous wrong majority at least once
  — but conformity **collapses when even one person dissents.** [EVIDENCE] The raw
  mechanism a manufactured consensus exploits (and why leaders must prevent visible
  dissent).
- **Deutsch & Gerard (1955)** distinguish **normative** influence (conform to avoid
  rejection) from informational. DOI 10.1037/h0046408. [EVIDENCE] Pinpoints the lever:
  **fear of peer rejection** drives compliance even absent any factual uncertainty.
- **Leymann (1990), mobbing**, *Violence and Victims* 5(2). DOI 10.1891/0886-6708.5.2.119.
  [EVIDENCE] The clinical name for **coworkers "ganging up"** to push out a target —
  making the exit look "voluntary," no formal managerial act required. ("Driven out and
  burnt on a stake.")
- **O'Reilly, Robinson, Berdahl & Banki (2015), "Is Negative Attention Better Than No
  Attention?", *Organization Science* 26(3).** DOI 10.1287/orsc.2014.0900. [EVIDENCE]
  Ostracism is reported *more often* than harassment, rated *more socially acceptable*,
  and **uniquely predicts actual turnover three years later** — the quiet, deniable way
  a group ejects someone. Bridges mechanisms 2 and 4.

## 3. Deniable enforcement / proxies → **moral disengagement & "dirty work"**

- **Bandura (1999), "Moral Disengagement," *PSPR* 3(3):193–209.** DOI
  10.1207/s15327957pspr0303_3. [EVIDENCE/theory] **Displacement** ("just the process")
  and **diffusion** of responsibility (spread blame so no one owns it) are *exactly*
  how leaders authorize coercion without feeling — or appearing — responsible, and how
  proxies carry it out with a clear conscience.
- **Hughes (1962), "Good People and Dirty Work," *Social Problems* 10(1).** DOI
  10.2307/799402. [OPINION/theory] "Good people" delegate the dirty work to agents they
  can disavow, then avert their eyes — the in-group's silence is itself the
  authorization. The classic articulation of **clean-hands-via-proxy.**
- **Ashforth & Kreiner (1999), "Dirty Work…," *AMR* 24(3).** DOI 10.5465/amr.1999.2202129.
  [EVIDENCE/theory] The *proxies* who do coercive dirty work form tight in-group
  subcultures that rationalize it — the sociology of the "cat's paw" / enforcer clique.
- **Sutton (2010), *Good Boss, Bad Boss*** — the **"umbrella vs. funnel"** framing:
  good bosses *absorb* pressure from above and shield their people; **deniable
  enforcement is the funnel with the leader's name filed off.** [OPINION] The exact
  inversion of the "shit umbrella" the observation names.
- *(Framing only)* **Herman & Chomsky (1988), *Manufacturing Consent*** — consent can
  be *engineered* to look spontaneous. [OPINION — contested framework] Analogy for
  astroturfed internal consensus; label as frame, not evidence.

## 4. Exit vilification + reputational retaliation → **work-devotion, retaliation, blacklisting**

- **Blair-Loy (2003), *Competing Devotions*** — the **work-devotion schema** casts
  career as a calling deserving single-minded allegiance; it is "compelling and
  coercive," framing anything less than total commitment (including exit) as **betrayal
  of a sacred obligation.** [EVIDENCE] The cultural substrate of "disloyal, never
  belonged." (Guild analog: "we only want dedicated, loyal team players.")
- **Reid (2015), "…the Ideal Worker Image," *Organization Science* 26(4).** DOI
  10.1287/orsc.2015.0975. [EVIDENCE] Those who couldn't embrace total devotion either
  *passed* (hid it — rated fine) or *revealed* it (**penalized in evaluations and more
  likely to exit**). Deviation from total devotion is stigmatized and career-damaging.
- **Near & Miceli (1985)** and **Rehg, Miceli, Near & Van Scotter (2008), *Organization
  Science* 19(2).** DOI 10.1287/orsc.1070.0310. [EVIDENCE] Organizations **retaliate**
  against those who break ranks; retaliation is patterned and **falls hardest on the
  least powerful** — the low-power person absorbs the reprisal, exactly as in the guild.
- **Starr, Prescott & Bishara (2021), "Noncompete Agreements in the U.S. Labor Force,"
  *J. Law & Economics* 64(1).** DOI 10.1086/712206. [EVIDENCE, n=11,505] The
  **formal-legal arm of exit control**: ~18% of workers currently bound, ~38% have
  signed; they appear even where legally **unenforceable** (an *in-terrorem* effect that
  functions as de facto blacklisting). The corporate "you'll be blacklisted on the
  server," in contract form.

## How the dynamic operates at work

1. A **devotion norm** makes wanting a boundary — let alone leaving — read as
   disloyalty (Blair-Loy, Reid). Exit is pre-stigmatized.
2. Control is **relocated to peers** (concertive/clan/normative) so the group enforces
   effort and conformity harder than management would (Barker, Sewell), powered by the
   universal **fear of rejection** (Asch, Deutsch & Gerard).
3. When someone must be pressured or pushed out, it happens as **peer ostracism/mobbing**
   — deniable, "voluntary-looking," and predictive of real exit (Leymann, O'Reilly).
4. Leadership keeps clean hands via **diffusion/displacement of responsibility** and
   **delegated dirty work** (Bandura, Hughes) — the funnel with the name filed off.
5. Those who leave anyway are **retaliated against**, reputationally and sometimes
   legally (Rehg et al., Starr et al.), so exit is costly enough to deter the next person.

## What corroborates / what complicates

- **Supports:** the concertive-control match is exceptionally tight and heavily
  evidenced; every mechanism has empirical backing (ethnography, experiments, validated
  scales, large surveys).
- **Complicates it (see `synthesis.md`):** peer/clan control and strong norms are often
  **genuinely healthy and high-performing** — Barker's own subjects initially *chose*
  and valued the team system; the pathology is **intensification + inescapability +
  orchestration-with-deniability**, not team-based control as such. Some reputation-
  sharing about ex-workers is legitimate (truthful, proportionate). And exit is
  **socially contagious** (Felps et al. 2009, turnover contagion) — so leadership's fear
  of exit isn't pure paranoia, and peer influence on leaving runs *both* ways.
